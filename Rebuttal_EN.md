# IEEE S&P 2027 Text Rebuttal — Working Draft

We thank the reviewers for recognizing LAMP's core idea and identifying where our claims and interface were imprecise. We agree that related-work comparison and input-encoding validity are the two highest-priority issues.

**Positioning and comparison.** LAMP is a specialized online checker for certified encoded matrix-product commitments, not a general-purpose IOP or end-to-end zkML system. Ligero, Brakedown, Blaze, and Orion provide related code-testing foundations but prove more general relations; zkCNN, zkGPT, and zkLLM target broader neural-network computations. We will compare their statements and input models conceptually rather than equate end-to-end timings, and clarify that LAMP's contribution is its matrix-specific reduction, not code testing itself.

We chose Freivalds-in-Groth16 under the same relation, curve, backend, hardware, and security parameters; it isolates our reduction but is not a state-of-the-art baseline. zkMatrix reports `O(k^2)` prover work and `O(log k)` transcript/verifier. DualMatrix reports `O(K+k)` field operations plus `O(k)` group operations/pairings, where `K=nnz(A)+nnz(B)+nnz(C)=Theta(k^2)` for dense inputs, and `O(log k)` transcript/verifier. zkMaP reports `O(k^2)` prover work, 320-byte proofs, and a constant pairing count, although its detailed analysis includes `O(k)` verifier-side field work. At fixed code rate, LAMP has `O(k^2)` field work for its intermediate vectors and `O(k^2)` encoded-commitment group work, but reduces the in-circuit relation to `O(tk + E_code)` constraints. As of August 26, we found no public zkMatrix implementation, and zkMaP's artifact link was unavailable; we contacted the authors and evaluated the public follow-up DualMatrix. On dense matrices at `k=1024`, we measured DualMatrix/LAMP proving times of `19.06/7.11 s`, verification times of `0.15/0.06 s`, and proofs of `88,844/43,332 B`; LAMP was lower on all three metrics at every tested size from `k=128` to `k=8,192`. We will add these results and a table of statements, operation types, circuit/verifier costs, proof size, setup, assumptions, and input model, documenting hardware, curve, security, implementation, and cost-boundary differences.

**Validity of the committed matrix encodings.** The reviewers are correct: the current online relation applies `EncCheck` only to the intermediate vectors, and validity of the row-wise encodings of `A,B,C` is not discharged by LAMP itself. The sampled folds do not replace this requirement. Our intended setting was a certified-input interface, but the manuscript did not define this boundary clearly enough. We will define a certification relation `R_cert` whose accepted proof establishes the existence of matrices and openings such that `rtABC` commits to their valid row-wise encodings. For proof-based certification, the revised theorem will compose the errors as `epsilon_total <= epsilon_cert + epsilon_LAMP`. An alternative trusted-registration instantiation instead makes encoder/registrar honesty an explicit assumption; we will not conflate that trust assumption with cryptographic certification. Without either interface, arbitrary roots are outside the claimed language. We will state this boundary throughout the abstract, construction, and security analysis.

If raw matrices must also be used inside the same SNARK, checking all `3k` encoded rows against their raw rows with our barycentric Reed--Solomon check adds `O(k(k+n)) = O(k^2)` constraints at fixed rate, eliminating the linear-circuit advantage. A reusable certification proof can bind them once, but amortization applies only when roots are reused. This bridge matters in end-to-end zkML when weights or activations feed nonlinear layers. We will describe LAMP as an online checker for already-certified commitments, separate certification from online measurements, and treat authentication of `rtABC` as application-level provenance.

**Security scope and setup.** The theorem proves the public-coin interactive protocol. We will specify the two-stage transcript order and domain separation, and limit the non-interactive claim to what is justified in the random-oracle model. We will also document Groth16's per-circuit setup and QALink's configuration-dependent CRS, reporting their costs or listing them as deployment limitations.

**Measurements and presentation.** The correct values are `0.06 s`, `8.43x`, and `1.77x`; we will fix all inconsistent occurrences. “Linear” refers to the main constraint count, not total prover time: intermediate computation and encoded-column commitments remain quadratic at fixed code rate. We will report the GPT-2 result as a trade-off—`1.77x` faster proving, but `6.52 s` versus `0.49 s` verification and `3.34 MB` versus `196 B` proofs—and explain that Merkle openings and CP-Link cause the larger proof. We will also clarify encoding-time accounting, add repetitions/variance and memory where available, and strengthen the proof of Theorem 6.2 and its appendix pointer.
