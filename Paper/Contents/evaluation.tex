\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Implementation Setup}

We implement $\protocol$ in Rust.
We evaluate two backend configurations for $\protocol$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{$\protocol$(Ped)}: a transparent SNARK backend using Merkle-tree-based (Pedersen) commitments.
    The large proof sizes observed in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} ($\approx 1.70$--$48.79$\,MB) are characteristic of such schemes, which trade proof size for transparent setup and avoid a structured reference string.
  \item \textbf{$\protocol$(KZG)}: a pairing-based SNARK backend using KZG commitments.
    This variant requires a structured setup but achieves comparable proving times with the same $\mathcal{O}(k)$ circuit scaling.
\end{itemize}
For encoding, we evaluate two configurations:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Linear-time encodable code} (used in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}).
    We instantiate $\mathcal{C}$ with an expander-based code~\cite{golovnev2023brakedown} of constant rate and relative distance $\delta$.
    The encoding constraints inside the SNARK circuit are $\mathcal{O}(k)$, yielding a total circuit size of $\mathcal{O}(tk)$.
  \item \textbf{Constant-rate code} (used in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding}).
    We sweep over code rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$ to characterize the encoding constraint overhead $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2/\rho)$, which dominates for large $k$ under general codes.
\end{itemize}
We compare both variants against a direct SNARK-in-circuit implementation of Freivalds' algorithm~\cite{freivalds1977probabilistic}, where the vector-matrix products $\vect{r}\mat{A}$, $\vect{x}\mat{B}$, and $\vect{r}\mat{C}$ are fully arithmetized, incurring $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ R1CS constraints.
The Freivalds baseline uses a pairing-based SNARK backend (consistent with its constant $0.001\,\text{s}$ verification time).
All experiments use matrices over a 256-bit prime field with $k$ ranging from $2^7$ to $2^{12}$.

\subsection{Constraint Count Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-constraints}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Figure~\ref{fig:constraints} report constraint counts for all three configurations.
The asymptotic gap between the two approaches is clearly visible.
For Freivalds, constraints grow by a factor of approximately $4\times$ with each doubling of $k$---a signature of $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ scaling---while both $\protocol$ variants grow by approximately $2\times$, confirming $\mathcal{O}(k)$ scaling under the linear-time encodable code.

The crossover in absolute constraint count occurs at $k = 256$ ($\log k = 8$):
below this threshold, Freivalds' $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ constant is sufficiently small that its circuit is marginally smaller ($49{,}151$ vs.\ $53{,}727$ for $\protocol$(Ped) at $\log k = 7$);
above it, $\protocol$ is consistently more compact.
At $k = 1{,}024$ ($\log k = 10$), $\protocol$(Ped) uses $420{,}197$ constraints versus $3{,}145{,}727$ for Freivalds---a $7.5\times$ reduction.
At $k = 2{,}048$ ($\log k = 11$), Freivalds exhausts available memory and cannot be run, while both $\protocol$ variants scale to at least $k = 4{,}096$ ($\log k = 12$) with under $1.68$\,M constraints.
This demonstrates that $\protocol$ extends the practical range of SNARK-based matrix multiplication verification by at least one order of magnitude in matrix dimension.

\subsection{Proving Time}
\label{sec:eval-proving}

Figure~\ref{fig:provetime} and Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} show the prove-time crossover between $\log k = 7$ ($k = 128$) and $\log k = 8$ ($k = 256$).
At $\log k = 7$, Freivalds is still faster ($0.67\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $1.07\,\text{s}$ for $\protocol$(Ped) and $2.74\,\text{s}$ for $\protocol$(KZG)).
At $\log k = 8$, $\protocol$(Ped) is already faster ($2.32\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $3.02\,\text{s}$ for Freivalds), and the advantage compounds rapidly as $k$ grows:
at $\log k = 9$, the speedup reaches $2.8\times$ ($5.84\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $16.28\,\text{s}$);
at $\log k = 10$, it reaches $23.9\times$ ($17.48\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $417.76\,\text{s}$).
Beyond $\log k = 10$, Freivalds is infeasible, while $\protocol$(Ped) completes $\log k = 12$ in $193.50\,\text{s}$ and $\protocol$(KZG) in $117.39\,\text{s}$.

We note that at $\log k = 7$, Freivalds proves faster due to two factors:
(i)~the per-constraint overhead of $\protocol$ includes Merkle-path hashing, which contributes fixed overhead per query;
and (ii)~the $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ constant in Freivalds' circuit is sufficiently small at this scale.
For $k \geq 256$, these constants are dominated by the circuit size difference, and both $\protocol$ variants become decisively faster.
Notably, $\protocol$(KZG) achieves faster proving than $\protocol$(Ped) at larger $k$ ($45.47\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $57.18\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 11$; $117.39\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $193.50\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 12$), reflecting the efficiency of the pairing-based backend for larger circuits.

\subsection{Verification Time and Proof Size}
\label{sec:eval-verify}

A trade-off of both $\protocol$ variants is that verification time and proof size are larger than the pairing-based Freivalds baseline.
Freivalds verification is constant at $0.001\,\text{s}$ for all tested~$k$---consistent with constant-time pairing-based SNARK verification---with a proof size of only $168$ bytes.
$\protocol$(Ped)'s verification time grows from $0.06\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 7$ to $1.38\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 12$,
approximately doubling with each doubling of $k$.
$\protocol$(KZG) exhibits a similar trend, growing from $0.23\,\text{s}$ to $1.37\,\text{s}$ over the same range.
This growth reflects the increase in public input size: the public statement $\stmt$ includes the $t$ queried column openings, whose total size scales as $\mathcal{O}(tk)$.

Proof sizes for $\protocol$(Ped) grow from $1.70\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 7$) to $48.79\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 12$),
roughly doubling with each unit increase in $\log k$---consistent with the $\mathcal{O}(k)$ witness size dominated by the opened codeword columns and their Merkle authentication paths.
$\protocol$(KZG) exhibits comparable proof sizes ($1.76$--$48.36\,\text{MB}$), as the dominant contribution comes from the commitment opening paths rather than the SNARK backend.

We remark that the proof size and verification time of $\protocol$ can be further improved by replacing the Merkle-based commitment with a fully algebraic vector commitment scheme (e.g., KZG~\cite{kate2010constant} applied directly to the commitment layer), which could reduce proof size to $\mathcal{O}(\lambda)$ and verification to $\mathcal{O}(1)$ at the cost of a structured setup.
We leave this optimization as future engineering work.

\subsection{Encoding Cost Analysis}
\label{sec:eval-encoding}

Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} reports the precomputation and encoding times under general constant-rate codes for $\log k \in [10, 20]$ and rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$.
Two observations are noteworthy.

\paragraph{Encoding is fast.}
Even at $\log k = 20$ ($k = 2^{20} \approx 10^6$), encoding completes in $0.623\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/2$) and $0.680\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/4$), and precomputation takes at most $0.220\,\text{s}$.
These costs are negligible relative to the native $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ matrix multiplication, confirming that encoding does not introduce a new bottleneck in the prover's pipeline.

\paragraph{Rate vs.\ constraint trade-off.}
The encoding constraints---which contribute $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2(1-\rho)/\rho)$ to the circuit---grow roughly as $2\times$ when the rate is halved, as expected.
At $\log k = 16$, rate $1/2$ yields $328{,}558$ encoding constraints while rate $1/4$ yields $591{,}578$ constraints ($1.8\times$ more).
For the Merkle-hash component (fold and path checks), the constraint count is $\mathcal{O}(tk)$ and appears in the $\protocol$ columns of Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}; the encoding constraints in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} represent an \emph{additional} overhead incurred when using general codes instead of linear-time encodable codes.

When the encoding constraint cost is acceptable (i.e., when $k^2$ fits within the SNARK's capacity), rate $1/2$ represents a good default, offering a balanced trade-off between code distance $\delta$ (which determines the soundness error per query) and encoding constraint overhead.
For applications demanding a higher soundness level per query (larger $\delta$), a lower rate with more queries may be preferable.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} together establish the following conclusions about $\protocol$.

\paragraph{Constraint efficiency.}
Both $\protocol$ variants achieve $\mathcal{O}(k)$ circuit growth (under linear-time encodable codes), versus $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ for Freivalds-based SNARK arithmetization.
The constraint advantage reaches $7.5\times$ at $k = 1{,}024$ and grows without bound.
Freivalds is infeasible beyond $k = 1{,}024$ under the tested configuration; both $\protocol$ variants scale to at least $k = 4{,}096$.

\paragraph{Proving time.}
Both $\protocol$ variants outperform Freivalds in prove time for $k \geq 256$ ($\log k = 8$), with speedups reaching $23.9\times$ for $\protocol$(Ped) and $23.3\times$ for $\protocol$(KZG) at $k = 1{,}024$.
For $k = 128$ ($\log k = 7$), Freivalds proves faster due to its lower per-constraint overhead at small circuit sizes.
Between the two $\protocol$ variants, $\protocol$(KZG) achieves faster proving at larger $k$, reaching $1.65\times$ speedup over $\protocol$(Ped) at $\log k = 12$.

\paragraph{Verification and proof size.}
$\protocol$'s verification is slower ($0.06$--$1.38\,\text{s}$ for Ped; $0.23$--$1.37\,\text{s}$ for KZG) and proofs are larger ($1.70$--$48.79\,\text{MB}$) than Freivalds ($0.001\,\text{s}$, $168$ bytes).
These costs reflect the commitment opening overhead and can be further improved by applying algebraic commitment schemes directly to the commitment layer.

\paragraph{Practical recommendation.}
For verifiable ML inference tasks involving matrices with $k \geq 256$---the regime of practical interest for modern Transformer layers---$\protocol$ is the preferred choice.
$\protocol$(KZG) offers the best proving time, while $\protocol$(Ped) provides a transparent setup without a structured reference string.
Their combination of linear circuit growth, competitive proving time, and manageable verifier work makes $\protocol$ well-suited for deployment scenarios where the prover (inference server) bears the computation cost and the verifier (client) requires only a succinct check.
