\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Implementation Setup}

We implement the multiplication-checking layer of $\protocol$ in Rust.
We evaluate three commitment and circuit-backend configurations for this layer:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{$\fmMerkle$}: a transparent SNARK backend using Merkle-tree commitments implemented with Pedersen hashes.
    This variant avoids a structured reference string and trades proof size for transparent setup.
  \item \textbf{$\fmKZG$}: a pairing-based SNARK backend (Groth16) using KZG commitments.
    This variant requires a structured setup but achieves fast proving at larger circuit sizes due to the efficiency of the pairing-based backend.
  \item \textbf{$\fmCPLink$}: a hybrid configuration using a Groth16 circuit backend with Merkle-tree commitments and an explicit CP-SNARK linking mechanism.
    The Groth16 proof component is constant-sized ($260$ bytes), while the CP-SNARK link and Merkle opening paths constitute the bulk of the proof.
\end{itemize}
For encoding, we evaluate two configurations:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Conditional linear-time backend model} (used in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}).
    We instantiate $\mathcal{C}$ with an expander-based code~\cite{golovnev2023brakedown} of constant rate and relative distance $\delta$.
    Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} should be read as a conditional
    linear-time backend configuration: the reported constraint counts cover the
    implemented fold, opening, and linking circuit terms of the
    multiplication-checking layer, while the asymptotic claim assumes that the
    selected $\codeComV$ contributes $E_{\mathsf{cc}}(k)=\mathcal{O}(k)$.
    The artifact does not yet isolate or independently measure
    $\codeComV$ as a standalone component.
    Dense generator-matrix codeword-validity checks are not included in these
    rows; Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} reports that additional cost for
    general dense-code instantiations.
  \item \textbf{Constant-rate code} (used in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding}).
    We sweep over code rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$ to characterize the
    dense backend codeword-validity overhead
    $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2/\rho)$, which dominates for large
    $k$ under general dense-code instantiations.
\end{itemize}
We compare all three variants against an optimized SNARK-in-circuit implementation of Freivalds' algorithm~\cite{freivalds1977probabilistic}, where the vector-matrix products $\vect{r}\mat{A}$, $\vect{x}\mat{B}$, and $\vect{r}\mat{C}$ are fully arithmetized, incurring $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ R1CS constraints.
The optimized Freivalds implementation extends the feasible range to $\log k = 11$ ($k = 2{,}048$), compared to the earlier implementation which was limited to $\log k = 10$.
All experiments use matrices over a 256-bit prime field with $k$ ranging from $2^7$ to $2^{12}$.

\paragraph{Security and code parameters.}
Table~\ref{tab:eval-parameters} summarizes the parameters recorded by the
benchmark artifacts.
The main matrix-multiplication experiments use rate $\rho=1/2$, codeword length
$n=2k$, and $t=128$ sampled positions.
These rows are scaling measurements for the implemented checking layer, not a
complete deployment parameter certificate.
The security theorem in Section~\ref{sec:security} uses
$t=\lceil(\lambda+2)/\log_2(1/(1-\delta))\rceil$ so that the four-way proximity
union bound is at most $2^{-\lambda}$.
For an ideal rate-$1/2$ distance calculation with $\delta=1/2$, the reported
$t=128$ rows give a proximity term $4(1/2)^{128}=2^{-126}$; a strict
128-bit proximity term would use $t=130$.
If the selected expander-code backend certifies a smaller $\delta$, then $t$
must be increased according to the displayed formula.
Moving from $t=128$ to $t=130$ changes the $t$-linear fold/opening terms by
$1.6\%$, so it does not change the observed scaling trend.

\paragraph{Timing scope.}
The proving-time columns in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} report online
proving time and exclude one-time setup/key-generation time.
For the $\protocol$ artifact, total proving time includes matrix commitment,
vector commitment, circuit proving, and CPLink/KZG-style linking when present;
native matrix multiplication and setup are recorded separately.
For the Freivalds baseline, proving time likewise excludes native matrix
computation and setup.
The experiment logs record setup separately: for example, the CPLink
configuration ranges from $7.27\,\text{s}$ at $\log k=7$ to
$171.40\,\text{s}$ at $\log k=12$, while the Freivalds setup ranges from
$5.45\,\text{s}$ at $\log k=7$ to $1303.91\,\text{s}$ at $\log k=11$.
Native matrix computation, matrix commitment, vector commitment, circuit
proving, linking, and verification are recorded as separate columns in the
artifact CSVs, but the current artifact does not yet log peak memory or timing
variance.

\paragraph{Artifact limitations.}
The current benchmark artifact is sufficient to support the scaling claims for
the implemented multiplication-checking layer in
Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}, but it does not yet provide a standalone
$\codeComV$ implementation, a per-component constraint breakdown, peak resident
memory, or repeated-run statistics.
Accordingly, we report Freivalds' $\log k=12$ failure as an observed
out-of-memory outcome in the benchmark environment, not as a fitted memory
scaling law.
These missing measurements are orthogonal to the soundness theorem but are
important for a complete systems evaluation.

\subsection{Constraint Count Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-constraints}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Figure~\ref{fig:constraints} report constraint counts for all four configurations.
The asymptotic gap between the two approaches is clearly visible.
For Freivalds, constraints grow by a factor of approximately $4\times$ with each doubling of $k$---a signature of $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ scaling---while all three $\protocol$ variants grow by approximately $2\times$, confirming $\mathcal{O}(k)$ scaling of the implemented checking layer under the conditional linear-time backend model.

The crossover in absolute constraint count occurs at $k = 256$ ($\log k = 8$):
below this threshold, Freivalds' $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ constant is sufficiently small that its circuit is marginally smaller ($49{,}610$ vs.\ $53{,}727$ for $\fmMerkle$ at $\log k = 7$);
above it, all $\protocol$ variants are consistently more compact.
At $k = 1{,}024$ ($\log k = 10$), $\fmMerkle$ uses $420{,}197$ constraints versus $3{,}156{,}298$ for Freivalds---a $7.5\times$ reduction.
At $k = 2{,}048$ ($\log k = 11$), the gap widens to $15.1\times$: $\fmCPLink$ requires only $835{,}065$ constraints versus $12{,}622{,}154$ for Freivalds.
Beyond $\log k = 11$, Freivalds exhausts available memory in the benchmark environment, while all $\protocol$ variants scale to at least $k = 4{,}096$ ($\log k = 12$) with under $1.68$\,M constraints.
This confirms that $\protocol$ extends the practical range of SNARK-based matrix multiplication verification by at least one order of magnitude in matrix dimension.

\subsection{Proving Time}
\label{sec:eval-proving}

Figure~\ref{fig:provetime} and Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} show the prove-time comparison across all four configurations.
The crossover point---at which $\protocol$ variants begin to outperform Freivalds---varies by variant.
$\fmMerkle$ achieves crossover at $\log k = 8$ ($2.32\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $2.36\,\text{s}$), while $\fmKZG$ and $\fmCPLink$ cross over between $\log k = 9$ and $\log k = 10$.

At $\log k = 7$, Freivalds is faster than all $\protocol$ variants ($0.66\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $1.07\,\text{s}$ for $\fmMerkle$, $2.74\,\text{s}$ for $\fmKZG$, and $1.98\,\text{s}$ for $\fmCPLink$).
As $k$ grows, the $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ circuit cost of Freivalds dominates:
at $\log k = 10$, $\fmMerkle$ achieves $1.71\times$ speedup ($17.48\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $29.89\,\text{s}$);
at $\log k = 11$, the speedup reaches $2.06\times$ for $\fmMerkle$ ($57.18\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $117.89\,\text{s}$), $2.59\times$ for $\fmKZG$ ($45.47\,\text{s}$), and $2.95\times$ for $\fmCPLink$ ($40.03\,\text{s}$).
Beyond $\log k = 11$, Freivalds is infeasible in the benchmark environment due to memory exhaustion, while all $\protocol$ variants complete $\log k = 12$: $\fmKZG$ in $117.39\,\text{s}$, $\fmMerkle$ in $193.50\,\text{s}$, and $\fmCPLink$ in $200.19\,\text{s}$.

We note that at small $k$ ($\log k = 7$), Freivalds proves faster due to two factors:
(i)~the per-constraint overhead of $\protocol$ includes Merkle-path hashing and CP-SNARK linking, which contribute fixed overhead per query;
and (ii)~the $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ constant in Freivalds' circuit is sufficiently small at this scale.
For $k \geq 256$, these constants are progressively dominated by the circuit size difference.
Among the $\protocol$ variants, $\fmKZG$ achieves the fastest proving at larger $k$ ($45.47\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $57.18\,\text{s}$ for $\fmMerkle$ and $40.03\,\text{s}$ for $\fmCPLink$ at $\log k = 11$; $117.39\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $193.50\,\text{s}$ and $200.19\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 12$), reflecting the efficiency of the pairing-based backend for larger circuits.
$\fmCPLink$ achieves competitive circuit-level proving (its Groth16 circuit proof time is as low as $0.81$--$16.15\,\text{s}$), but its total prove time at $\log k = 12$ is dominated by the matrix commitment phase ($174.45\,\text{s}$ out of $200.19\,\text{s}$).

\subsection{Verification Time and Proof Size}
\label{sec:eval-verify}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} reports verification times for all four configurations.
$\fmMerkle$ achieves the fastest verification among the $\protocol$ variants, growing from $0.06\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 7$ to $1.38\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 12$, approximately doubling with each doubling of $k$.
$\fmKZG$ exhibits a similar trend ($0.23$--$1.37\,\text{s}$).
$\fmCPLink$ incurs higher verification overhead ($0.49$--$2.83\,\text{s}$), dominated by its CP-SNARK link verification component ($0.47$--$2.52\,\text{s}$).
This growth reflects the cost of linking the $t$ queried encoded columns to the
committed objects; the linked column material scales as $\mathcal{O}(tk)$ for
fixed security parameter.

The optimized Freivalds baseline exhibits verification times that grow from $0.01\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 7$ to $2.36\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 11$, scaling roughly as $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ with the circuit size.
At $\log k = 11$, $\fmMerkle$ verification ($0.64\,\text{s}$) is $3.7\times$ faster than Freivalds ($2.36\,\text{s}$), demonstrating that the $\mathcal{O}(k)$ circuit structure of $\protocol$ yields a verification advantage at sufficiently large $k$.

Proof sizes for all $\protocol$ variants are comparable:
$\fmMerkle$ grows from $1.70\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 7$) to $48.79\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 12$),
$\fmKZG$ from $1.76$ to $48.36\,\text{MB}$,
and $\fmCPLink$ from $1.72$ to $48.32\,\text{MB}$---all roughly doubling with each unit increase in $\log k$, consistent with the $\mathcal{O}(k)$ witness size dominated by the opened codeword columns and their Merkle authentication paths.
For $\fmCPLink$, the proof comprises a constant-sized Groth16 component ($260$ bytes), Merkle opening paths ($160$--$260\,\text{KB}$), and a CP-SNARK link proof ($1.57$--$48.07\,\text{MB}$); the CP-SNARK link constitutes over $90\%$ of the total proof size.

We remark that the proof size and verification time of $\protocol$ can be further improved by replacing the Merkle-based commitment with a fully algebraic vector commitment scheme (e.g., KZG~\cite{kate2010constant} applied directly to the commitment layer), which could reduce proof size to $\mathcal{O}(\lambda)$ and verification to $\mathcal{O}(1)$ at the cost of a structured setup.
We leave this optimization as future engineering work.

\subsection{Encoding Cost Analysis}
\label{sec:eval-encoding}

Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} reports the precomputation and encoding times under general constant-rate codes for $\log k \in [10, 20]$ and rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$.
Two observations are noteworthy.

\paragraph{Encoding is fast.}
Even at $\log k = 20$ ($k = 2^{20} \approx 10^6$), encoding completes in $0.623\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/2$) and $0.680\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/4$), and precomputation takes at most $0.220\,\text{s}$.
These costs are negligible relative to the native $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ matrix multiplication, confirming that encoding does not introduce a new bottleneck in the prover's pipeline.

\paragraph{Rate vs.\ constraint trade-off.}
The dense codeword-validity constraints---which contribute $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2(1-\rho)/\rho)$ to the circuit under dense generator-matrix checks---grow roughly as $2\times$ when the rate is halved, as expected.
At $\log k = 16$, rate $1/2$ yields $328{,}558$ backend constraints while rate $1/4$ yields $591{,}578$ constraints ($1.8\times$ more).
For the Merkle-hash component (fold and path checks), the constraint count is $\mathcal{O}(tk)$ and appears in the $\protocol$ columns of Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}; the constraints in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} represent an \emph{additional} backend codeword-validity overhead incurred when using general dense codes instead of linear-time code-commitment backends.

When the backend codeword-validity cost is acceptable (i.e., when $k^2$ fits within the SNARK's capacity), rate $1/2$ represents a good default, offering a balanced trade-off between code distance $\delta$ (which determines the soundness error per query) and backend overhead.
For applications demanding a higher soundness level per query (larger $\delta$), a lower rate with more queries may be preferable.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} together establish the following conclusions about $\protocol$.

\paragraph{Constraint efficiency.}
All three $\protocol$ variants achieve $\mathcal{O}(k)$ checking-layer circuit
growth under the conditional linear-time code-commitment backend model, versus
$\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ for Freivalds-based SNARK arithmetization.
The measured checking-layer constraint advantage reaches $7.5\times$ at
$k = 1{,}024$ ($\log k = 10$) and $15.1\times$ at $k = 2{,}048$
($\log k = 11$).
Even with an optimized Freivalds implementation that extends to $\log k = 11$,
the baseline does not complete at $\log k = 12$ in our benchmark environment,
while all $\protocol$ variants scale to at least $k = 4{,}096$.

\paragraph{Proving time.}
$\fmMerkle$ outperforms Freivalds from $\log k = 8$ onward ($2.32\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $2.36\,\text{s}$);
$\fmKZG$ and $\fmCPLink$ cross over between $\log k = 9$ and $\log k = 10$.
At $\log k = 11$, speedups reach $2.06\times$ ($\fmMerkle$), $2.59\times$ ($\fmKZG$), and $2.95\times$ ($\fmCPLink$).
For $k = 128$ ($\log k = 7$), Freivalds proves faster due to its lower per-constraint overhead at small circuit sizes.
Among the $\protocol$ variants, $\fmKZG$ achieves the fastest proving at larger $k$, reaching $1.65\times$ speedup over $\fmMerkle$ at $\log k = 12$.

\paragraph{Verification and proof size.}
$\fmMerkle$ achieves the lowest verification overhead among $\protocol$ variants ($0.06$--$1.38\,\text{s}$), followed by $\fmKZG$ ($0.23$--$1.37\,\text{s}$) and $\fmCPLink$ ($0.49$--$2.83\,\text{s}$).
Freivalds verification grows from $0.01\,\text{s}$ ($\log k = 7$) to $2.36\,\text{s}$ ($\log k = 11$), scaling with its $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ circuit size.
At $\log k = 11$, $\fmMerkle$ verification ($0.64\,\text{s}$) is $3.7\times$ faster than Freivalds.
Proof sizes for all $\protocol$ variants range from $1.70$--$48.79\,\text{MB}$ and can be further improved by applying algebraic commitment schemes directly to the commitment layer.

\paragraph{Deployment regime.}
The crossover occurs at dimensions already common in large linear-algebra workloads:
$\fmMerkle$ becomes faster than Freivalds at $k = 256$, and all
$\protocol$ variants provide a large constraint advantage by $k = 1{,}024$.
This makes the protocol most attractive for large square or near-square matrix
products when the application can supply the required code-commitment backend
and tolerate the measured opening/linking material.

\paragraph{Practical recommendation.}
For verifiable matrix-multiplication workloads with $k \geq 256$, $\protocol$
is attractive under the certified-input and linear-time-backend assumptions.
$\fmKZG$ offers the best proving time, $\fmMerkle$ provides a transparent setup without a structured reference string, and $\fmCPLink$ achieves the highest speedup over Freivalds at the largest jointly feasible dimension ($2.95\times$ at $\log k = 11$) with a compact Groth16 circuit proof.
Their combination of linear circuit growth and competitive proving time makes
$\protocol$ most attractive in settings where the prover bears the native
matrix-arithmetic cost and the application can tolerate backend-dependent
opening and linking material.
