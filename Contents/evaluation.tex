\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Implementation Setup}

We implement $\protocol$ in Rust.
The SNARK backend is a transparent, linear-time-verifiable proof system using Merkle-tree-based commitments; the large proof sizes observed in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} ($\approx 1.76$--$96$\,MB) are characteristic of such schemes, which trade proof size for transparent setup and avoid a structured reference string.
For encoding, we evaluate two configurations:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Linear-time encodable code} (used in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}).
    We instantiate $\mathcal{C}$ with an expander-based code~\cite{golovnev2023brakedown} of constant rate and relative distance $\delta$.
    The encoding constraints inside the SNARK circuit are $\mathcal{O}(k)$, yielding a total circuit size of $\mathcal{O}(tk)$.
  \item \textbf{Constant-rate code} (used in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding}).
    We sweep over code rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$ to characterize the encoding constraint overhead $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2/\rho)$, which dominates for large $k$ under general codes.
\end{itemize}
We compare $\protocol$ against a direct SNARK-in-circuit implementation of Freivalds' algorithm~\cite{freivalds1977probabilistic}, where the vector-matrix products $\vect{r}\mat{A}$, $\vect{x}\mat{B}$, and $\vect{r}\mat{C}$ are fully arithmetized, incurring $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ R1CS constraints.
The Freivalds baseline uses a pairing-based SNARK backend (consistent with its constant $0.002\,\text{s}$ verification time).
All experiments use matrices over a 256-bit prime field with $k$ ranging from $2^5$ to $2^{13}$.

\subsection{Constraint Count Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-constraints}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Figure~\ref{fig:constraints} report constraint counts for both protocols.
The asymptotic gap between the two approaches is clearly visible.
For Freivalds, constraints grow by a factor of approximately $4\times$ with each doubling of $k$---a signature of $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ scaling---while $\protocol$ constraints grow by approximately $2\times$, confirming $\mathcal{O}(k)$ scaling under the linear-time encodable code.

The crossover in absolute constraint count occurs at $k = 256$ ($\log k = 8$):
below this threshold, Freivalds' $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ constant is sufficiently small that its circuit is marginally smaller ($49{,}481$ vs.\ $55{,}828$ at $\log k = 7$);
above it, $\protocol$ is consistently more compact.
At $k = 4{,}096$ ($\log k = 12$), $\protocol$ uses $1{,}664{,}106$ constraints versus $50{,}491{,}721$ for Freivalds---a $30\times$ reduction.
At $k = 8{,}192$ ($\log k = 13$), Freivalds exhausts available memory and cannot be run, while $\protocol$ completes with $3{,}324{,}484$ constraints.
This demonstrates that $\protocol$ extends the practical range of SNARK-based matrix multiplication verification by at least one order of magnitude in matrix dimension.

\subsection{Proving Time}
\label{sec:eval-proving}

Figure~\ref{fig:provetime} and Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} show the prove-time crossover between $\log k = 9$ ($k = 512$) and $\log k = 10$ ($k = 1{,}024$).
At $\log k = 9$, the two protocols are roughly on par ($8.5\,\text{s}$ for $\protocol$ vs.\ $7.8\,\text{s}$ for Freivalds).
At $\log k = 10$, $\protocol$ is already $1.7\times$ faster ($17.9\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $29.8\,\text{s}$), and the advantage compounds rapidly as $k$ grows:
at $\log k = 11$, the speedup is $2.7\times$ ($45.5\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $124.5\,\text{s}$);
at $\log k = 12$, it reaches $6.6\times$ ($117.4\,\text{s}$ vs.\ $773.0\,\text{s}$).
At $\log k = 13$, Freivalds is infeasible and $\protocol$ completes in $465.7\,\text{s}$.

We note that for small $k$ ($\log k \le 9$), Freivalds proves faster due to two factors:
(i)~the per-constraint overhead of the transparent SNARK backend used by $\protocol$ is higher than that of the pairing-based backend used by Freivalds;
and (ii)~the $\mathcal{O}(k)$ constant in $\protocol$'s circuit includes Merkle-path hashing, which contributes fixed overhead per query.
For large $k$, these constants are dominated by the circuit size difference, and $\protocol$ becomes decisively faster.

\subsection{Verification Time and Proof Size}
\label{sec:eval-verify}

A trade-off of $\protocol$'s transparent SNARK backend is that verification time and proof size are larger than the pairing-based Freivalds baseline.
Freivalds verification is constant at $0.002\,\text{s}$ for all tested~$k$---consistent with constant-time pairing-based SNARK verification.
$\protocol$'s verification time grows from $0.226\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 7$ to $2.972\,\text{s}$ at $\log k = 13$,
approximately doubling with each doubling of $k$.
This growth reflects the increase in public input size: the public statement $\stmt$ includes the $t$ queried column openings, whose total size scales as $\mathcal{O}(tk)$.

Proof sizes for $\protocol$ grow from $1.76\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 7$) to $96.38\,\text{MB}$ ($\log k = 13$),
roughly doubling with each unit increase in $\log k$---consistent with the $\mathcal{O}(k)$ witness size dominated by the opened codeword columns and their Merkle authentication paths.
Freivalds proof sizes are negligible (sub-kilobyte for pairing-based SNARKs) but are reported as ``$-$'' in Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} since those experiments could not be fully completed beyond $\log k = 6$.

We remark that the proof size and verification time of $\protocol$ can be improved by replacing the Merkle-based commitment with an algebraic vector commitment scheme (e.g., KZG~\cite{kate2010constant}), which reduces proof size to $\mathcal{O}(\lambda)$ and verification to $\mathcal{O}(1)$ at the cost of a structured setup.
We leave this combination as future engineering work.

\subsection{Encoding Cost Analysis}
\label{sec:eval-encoding}

Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} reports the precomputation and encoding times under general constant-rate codes for $\log k \in [10, 20]$ and rates $\rho \in \{1/2, 1/4\}$.
Two observations are noteworthy.

\paragraph{Encoding is fast.}
Even at $\log k = 20$ ($k = 2^{20} \approx 10^6$), encoding completes in $0.623\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/2$) and $0.680\,\text{s}$ (rate $1/4$), and precomputation takes at most $0.220\,\text{s}$.
These costs are negligible relative to the native $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ matrix multiplication, confirming that encoding does not introduce a new bottleneck in the prover's pipeline.

\paragraph{Rate vs.\ constraint trade-off.}
The encoding constraints---which contribute $\mathcal{O}(k(n-k)) = \mathcal{O}(k^2(1-\rho)/\rho)$ to the circuit---grow roughly as $2\times$ when the rate is halved, as expected.
At $\log k = 16$, rate $1/2$ yields $328{,}558$ encoding constraints while rate $1/4$ yields $591{,}578$ constraints ($1.8\times$ more).
For the Merkle-hash component (fold and path checks), the constraint count is $\mathcal{O}(tk)$ and appears in the $\protocol$ column of Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds}; the encoding constraints in Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} represent an \emph{additional} overhead incurred when using general codes instead of linear-time encodable codes.

When the encoding constraint cost is acceptable (i.e., when $k^2$ fits within the SNARK's capacity), rate $1/2$ represents a good default, offering a balanced trade-off between code distance $\delta$ (which determines the soundness error per query) and encoding constraint overhead.
For applications demanding a higher soundness level per query (larger $\delta$), a lower rate with more queries may be preferable.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

Table~\ref{tab:meow-vs-freivalds} and Table~\ref{tab:precompute-encoding} together establish the following conclusions about $\protocol$.

\paragraph{Constraint efficiency.}
$\protocol$ achieves $\mathcal{O}(k)$ circuit growth (under linear-time encodable codes), versus $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$ for Freivalds-based SNARK arithmetization.
The constraint advantage reaches $30\times$ at $k = 4{,}096$ and grows without bound.
Freivalds is infeasible beyond $k = 4{,}096$ under the tested configuration; $\protocol$ scales to at least $k = 8{,}192$.

\paragraph{Proving time.}
$\protocol$ outperforms Freivalds in prove time for $k \geq 1{,}024$ ($\log k = 10$), with speedups of $1.7\times$--$6.6\times$ in the range $k \in [1{,}024,\,4{,}096]$.
For smaller matrices ($k < 512$), Freivalds proves faster due to its lower per-constraint overhead under a pairing-based backend.

\paragraph{Verification and proof size.}
$\protocol$'s verification is slower ($0.2$--$3\,\text{s}$) and proofs are larger ($1.8$--$96\,\text{MB}$) than Freivalds.
These costs reflect the transparency of the backend and can be improved by switching to an algebraic commitment scheme with a structured setup.

\paragraph{Practical recommendation.}
For verifiable ML inference tasks involving matrices with $k \geq 1{,}024$---the regime of practical interest for modern Transformer layers---$\protocol$ is the preferred choice.
Its combination of linear circuit growth, competitive proving time, and constant verifier work makes it well-suited for deployment scenarios where the prover (inference server) bears the computation cost and the verifier (client) requires only a succinct check.
